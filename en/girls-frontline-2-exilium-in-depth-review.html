Girls' Frontline 2: Exilium Review: Turn-Based Tactics Worth Trying

A 3D tactical RPG with outstanding presentation, a squad system with real depth and a snappier rhythm than XCOM, held back by gacha and a grindy late-game upgrade loop.

Home › Reviews › Girls' Frontline 2: Exilium Review: Turn-Based Tactics Worth Trying
Mobile / PC · Tactical RPG · Review 2026-09-19 4 min read OtaHub Editorial
Quick verdict
Girls' Frontline 2: Exilium is worth trying if you like turn-based tactics, anime characters and squad optimisation. Cover, the Stability Index and support attacks create plenty of interesting decisions, though tactical depth is sometimes drowned out by stats, gear and gacha. Players in Vietnam get an official Vietnamese version (Lưu Đày) from August 27, 2026. OtaHub score: 8.5/10, Great.

Girls' Frontline 2: Exilium is a free-to-play 3D tactical RPG from MICA Team (Sunborn) and a sequel set in the world of Girls' Frontline. You return as the Commander, leading a squad of T-Dolls through contaminated zones after leaving Griffin & Kryuger. The global version launched on December 3, 2024 on PC and mobile, with Steam releases following on February 10, 2025. Vietnam has its own edition, Girls' Frontline 2: Lưu Đày, published by THS Game since August 27, 2026 and fully localised in Vietnamese.

GFL2’s appeal goes beyond its lavishly modelled cast. It folds positioning, breaking enemy protection and skill combos into an approachable gacha structure. The result has its own identity: tactical enough to make you think, without XCOM’s punishing hit chances and soldier management.

Story and world: a sequel that doesn’t lock out newcomers

The story picks up after the Commander cuts ties with Griffin & Kryuger. Instead of running a large military force, they take bounty contracts and travel with a T-Doll squad aboard the Elmo. A routine transport job drags the team into a wider conspiracy and reopens links to the past.

Players of the first game will recognise plenty of terms, factions and faces. Newcomers can still follow the main plot thanks to chapter-by-chapter introductions, but the density of names is high. Early pacing is slow and dialogue-heavy; in return, facial animation, cutscenes and voice acting give key scenes more weight than a static visual novel.

The game keeps its military sci-fi flavour: T-Dolls are not just collectibles but have memories, duties and their own views on humans. Chapter quality is uneven, though, and some exposition runs longer than it needs to, which can test players who just want to get into a fight.

Combat: XCOM on the surface, different underneath

Every mission plays out on a grid. You control a small squad, move within action limits, use cover and fire off skills to complete objectives. The XCOM comparison is fair given the camera, cover and flanking, but GFL2 sharply reduces random misses. The focus shifts to turn order, skill range, elemental effects and wearing down a target’s protection.

How do cover and the Stability Index work?

Units behind cover are protected, but that advantage is tied to the Stability Index. Attacks and skills reduce Stability; once it runs out, the target is destabilised and cover no longer protects it effectively. Good play usually comes in two steps: use a character with strong Stability Damage to open the target, then let your main damage dealer unload.

This gives every T-Doll a clear job. A unit with modest damage is still valuable if it breaks Stability quickly, provides support fire, pushes targets or buffs allies. Harder stages force you to read skills carefully instead of simply fielding your highest-rarity characters.

The fun is in the combos

Many characters have support attacks that trigger when an ally attacks under the right conditions. With a well-built squad, one action can chain into extra shots, crowd control or skill resource refunds. This is GFL2 at its best: you are not just asking “who hits hardest” but who opens the fight, who breaks Stability and who finishes the target.

Missions also vary, with objectives such as holding a position, surviving waves or extracting to a marked point. Not every map uses its terrain well, though. In farming stages, squad power and auto-battle gradually matter more than tactical decisions, so repetition creeps in later on.

Character building and gear: lots of options, lots of work

Each T-Doll has active skills, passives and a defined role. Weapons span assault rifles, handguns, shotguns, sniper rifles, machine guns, SMGs and melee. Beyond character and weapon levels you also manage Neural Helix, duplicates and weapon attachments.

Attachments add stats and set bonuses, opening up build optimisation. The downside is random substats that can drag out the grind. For most players, getting the right set bonus and main stats matters more than chasing a “perfect” roll. If you are just starting, our beginner’s guide will help you avoid wasting resources.

Gacha and economy: look closely before you invest

GFL2 is free to play and earns money from character, weapon and outfit pulls plus resource bundles. Limited banners have pity, but rates and rules can differ by banner type and version. Check Probability Details on the current banner before spending Collapse Pieces or tickets, rather than trusting an old number online.

Characters without duplicates can clear the story if properly invested, but duplicates and signature weapons can boost performance considerably. Because upgrade materials are split across many systems, raising too many characters at once can leave your account short. The safe approach is to finish one core squad, then expand as each mode demands.

Graphics, sound and character presentation

This is the game’s strongest suit. The 3D models keep an anime look while rendering metal, fabric, leather and gun detail convincingly. Cinematic camera work on ultimate skills is eye-catching without making battles hard to read. Small animations in menus, the lounge and character screens make the T-Dolls feel alive.

The soundtrack leans tense, industrial and post-apocalyptic, and gunfire has real weight. The PC interface is clear, while small phone screens make tile selection and long skill descriptions less comfortable. Performance depends on your device and settings, so don’t treat a single FPS result as representative of every machine.

The long-term loop and where it wears thin

After the story, the rhythm revolves around dailies, events, attachment farming and challenge modes. There are enough goals to keep optimisers coming back, but the to-do list piles up. When difficulty is low, auto-battle turns farming into a chore; when it rises, gaps in investment and character choice become obvious.

The lounge and Covenant system do a good job of building attachment to the characters, but they don’t replace a deep base-management mode. If you are here for tactics, combat and squad building are the core; the life-sim activities are mostly a bonus.

Strengths and things to weigh up

Strengths

  • Turn-based combat that is easy to grasp yet deep, thanks to Stability, cover and support combos.
  • Character models, skill animations and presentation among the best in 3D anime games.
  • Clear character roles and many ways to build a squad.
  • An official Vietnamese version with cross-play between phone and PC.

Things to weigh up

  • Character and weapon gacha add pressure on resource management.
  • Attachment farming is luck-based and repetitive.
  • A jargon-heavy opening and occasionally slow storytelling.
  • Less comfortable on small screens than on PC.

What critics and players say

On Metacritic the game has only two scored reviews, not enough for a Metascore: INVEN gave it 83/100, praising its expressive characters and tactical touches such as suppressive fire, while GameMAG gave it 80/100 and called its monetisation “more than tolerable”, noting you can get through half the game without trouble. In China, Gcores and Youxi Putao praised its aesthetics, animation and camera work, while some early players found it hard to learn and short on strategic depth.

Steam players are split: the Darkwinter version sits at about 70% positive across 3,305 reviews, while the HaoPlay version is around 54% across 3,439 (figures as of October 2, 2026). Commercially, the game drew more than 2 million players on its global launch day.

Who should play Girls' Frontline 2: Exilium?

Play it if you enjoy turn-based tactics, like building multiple squads and value strong character presentation. Fans of the Girls' Frontline world will get more out of it, but newcomers can comfortably start here.

Think twice if you dislike gacha, don’t want a daily login routine or expect a buy-once tactics game where everything unlocks through progress. GFL2 has good tactics, but its live-service structure directly shapes how fast your account grows.

FAQ

Is Girls' Frontline 2 just an anime XCOM?

Not quite. Both use grid maps, cover and flanking, but GFL2 focuses on character skills, the Stability Index and support chains; random misses and permadeath are not the point.

Does Girls' Frontline 2 have a Vietnamese version?

Yes. Girls' Frontline 2: Lưu Đày, published by THS Game, launched in Vietnam at 10:00 on August 27, 2026 on Android, iOS and PC, with a Vietnamese interface and story.

Do I need to play the first Girls' Frontline?

No. The sequel explains its main storyline for newcomers, although knowing the first game helps with the factions, returning characters and the Commander’s history.

Can free-to-play players keep up?

Yes, if you focus resources on one core squad and plan your pulls. Collecting many limited characters or signature weapons takes longer saving, or spending.

Should I play on PC or phone?

PC suits long sessions, reading skills and picking tiles precisely. Phones are handy for short tasks; performance and heat depend on the device.
